# How do you find out your stakeholders' why?

Ask them, and let them answer with a story instead of a number. Most of us know our own why cold. Very few of us know our employees', clients' or candidates' why, because the tools we use to ask either don't scale or turn their story into a score.

## Didn't Simon Sinek already solve this?

He taught us to lead with our own why. In Start With Why and his 2009 TED talk, Simon Sinek told us to start with why, and we listened. Most companies have a why now. It's on the website, the wall and slide two of the onboarding deck.

But that's our why. Finding our stakeholders' why is the part most of us skipped. And their why is the one that tells you how to serve them better. Why did the candidate turn down the offer? Why did the client renew, or walk? Why is your best manager quietly polishing up their LinkedIn profile?

I tell people that knowing your own why is the easy part. Knowing theirs is the work.

## Why does a feedback score lose the story?

Because a score asks for two translations, and something gets lost in each one.

Translation one happens in the stakeholder's head. Picture someone thinking: "My manager is great, but nobody told me this job was going to be this much travel." Then we hand them a scale and ask them to turn that story into a number. They pick a 3.

Translation two happens on your desk. Somebody reads that 3 and has to turn it back into a story. Was it the manager? The pay? The travel? They guess.

It's a game of telephone where one of the players is a spreadsheet. By the time the answer gets back to you, it isn't their story anymore. It's your best guess about their story.

## What gets lost in each translation?

A lot of what you'd act on. The number keeps how they felt, roughly. It drops what happened, who was involved and what would have changed their mind.

That's why a 3.8 out of 5 is so hard to use. Ten different stories can all land on a 3. Average them together and you get a number that describes no one in particular.

## Why haven't interviews and surveys been enough?

Because each one only does half the job.

Interviews get the why. Stay interviews, exit interviews, a cup of coffee. Real stories, in their own words. But nobody can sit down with 500 people.

Surveys scale. Everybody gets one. But they usually bring back numbers and an empty comment box. Writing a good answer from scratch is hard work, and people are busy.

One gets the why and doesn't scale. The other scales and rarely gets the why.

## What does asking for the why at scale look like?

It looks like a short interview that runs itself. Here's the shape, whatever tool you use:

1. Start from what you already know about the person. Don't ask their role if you have it.
2. Ask about the one thing you most need to understand, in plain words.
3. Let their answer pick the next question. For example, mark communication low, and the next question is about communication.
4. Make answering easy. Clear choices they can tap, plus room to say it in their own words when none of the choices fit.
5. Keep it short. Five good questions, about two minutes.
6. Read back stories, not averages.

Do that and nobody has to squeeze a story into a number, and nobody on your side has to guess what the number meant.

## What does that look like when it comes back?

It reads like this example, from a made-up sample staffing company: "The worker hit deadlines and quality, but needed close supervision on anything ambiguous. Communication was reliable on status, thin on raising risks early. The original job spec oversold the autonomy required. Recommend extending, with a clearer scope."

Every sentence in a write-up like that is something you can act on. Coach the risk-raising. Fix the job spec. Extend with a clearer scope. Try getting that out of a 3.8.

## How do you start with their why tomorrow?

Pick one group and one decision you need to make about them. Write down what you'd do differently depending on their answer. Then ask the fewest questions that would tell you, and let each answer steer the next.
