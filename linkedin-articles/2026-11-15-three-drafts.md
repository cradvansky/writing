# What does a 3.8 out of 5 actually tell you?

A 3.8 out of 5 tells you almost nothing. It tells you people were mostly fine and a little bit not, and it gives you zero clue which part was which. You can't coach a number. You can't fix a number. And you definitely can't quote a number back to the person who's paying you.

I tell people that a rating is where the conversation should start, not where it ends.

## Why is a 3.8 so hard to act on?

Because an average flattens every story into one digit. One person gave you a 2 because the onboarding was a mess. Another gave you a 5 because their manager is great. Spread them together like peanut butter and you get a 3.8 that describes nobody.

Then there's the open comment box. You know the one. "Anything else you'd like to share?" It stays empty. People are busy, and a blank box asks them to do the hardest part of the job: figure out what's worth saying and write it well.

So you end up with a score you can't defend and a comment field nobody filled out.

## What should you ask after someone gives a low score?

Ask about the one thing they just told you, not the next thing on your list. If someone rates communication a 2, the next question should be about communication. What happened? When? What would a 5 have looked like?

That sounds obvious. But most surveys ask the exact same next question no matter what the person just said. It's like a doctor hearing "my knee hurts" and replying "great, now let's talk about your eyesight."

## How many questions should a feedback survey ask?

Fewer than you think, as long as each one earns its spot. I'd take five good questions that follow the person's answers over twenty fixed ones that don't. Five is about two minutes of someone's time.

Here's the shape that works:

1. Start with what you already know about the person. Don't ask their role if you have it.
2. Ask one question about the thing you most need to understand.
3. Let the answer pick the next question.
4. Make it easy to answer. Clear choices beat a blank box. Don't ask for the moon and the stars on the first date.
5. Stop while they're still willing.

## What does good feedback look like when it comes back?

It reads like a short story, not a scoreboard. Something like: hit deadlines and quality, needed close supervision on anything unclear, reliable on status updates, thin on raising risks early, the original job description oversold how independent the role was.

Every sentence in that is something you can do something about. Coach the risk-raising. Fix the job description. Extend the person with a clearer scope. Compare that to "3.8, comments blank."

## Why does this matter more now?

Because I believe value is moving to the personal anecdote and the honest human read. Everyone has dashboards. Everyone has averages. What's rare is knowing why, in the words of the person who lived it.

# When should a small business stop buying software and have its own built?

When your team spends more time working around the software than working in it. That's the line. If someone re-types the same job from one system into another, chases status updates by phone, or rebuilds the same spreadsheet every Friday, the software isn't the problem. The gaps between your systems are, and no off-the-shelf tool was built to fill your gaps.

I tell people that their systems are usually fine. It's the work between them that is eating their week.

## How do you know your business has outgrown its tools?

Look for the workarounds. They're easy to spot once you start looking:

1. A spreadsheet that exists only to move data from one tool to another.
2. A person whose real job is "the one who knows how it works."
3. The same information typed into two or three places.
4. Status updates that happen by phone, text or "hey, quick question."
5. A report someone builds by hand every week, and nobody fully trusts.

My rule of thumb: if you counted three or more, you've outgrown your tools. Not because the tools are bad. Because your business does something specific and they were built for everybody.

## Why does off-the-shelf software end up running on spreadsheets anyway?

Because it was built for the average company, and nobody is the average company. Off-the-shelf software covers most of what you do. The rest is the part that makes you you, and it lives in a spreadsheet, a sticky note, or someone's head.

Buying a bigger, fancier tool rarely fixes it. You just get a bigger, fancier thing to work around.

## Isn't custom software only for big companies?

It used to be. A custom build often meant a $30-60K agency project, six months to a first version, and a change order every time you wanted something tweaked. Then version one sat there, frozen, while your business kept changing.

That math has flipped. AI made building fast. On the apps I run for clients today, the first version is live in month one, and the typical change goes from request to live in about 27 hours. That puts custom software in reach of a small business, not just a big one.

## What should you build first?

The one annoying task that happens every single week. Not the dream system. Not the everything-app. One app that does the boring work that falls between your existing tools, sitting beside the software you already run.

Good first builds look like:

1. An intake form that drops a new job straight into the system it belongs in.
2. A status page your customers can check instead of calling you.
3. The Friday report, built for you automatically.

Start small, get it live, and let it get better every month.

## Should you just build it yourselves?

I hear "we can do this ourselves" a lot. I love the ambition. The question is whether it's done right now. If not, who owns the project, how long will it take, and what stops while they work on it? Honestly, "we can do this ourselves" usually tells me they already see the value.

Far less than a $30-60K agency build, and I think it should be a flat monthly number, not a big bet up front. With RadWorks it's $1,500 a month. You get one app live in month one, and it gets better every month after.

# How do you practice a hard conversation before you have it?

You rehearse it out loud with something that pushes back. Reading about hard conversations doesn't prepare you for one. Watching a video doesn't either. The only thing that does is saying the words, hearing a real objection, and trying again until it stops feeling like the first time.

I tell people that knowledge is passive. Action is active. Training is no different.

## Why doesn't traditional training prepare people for hard conversations?

Because it teaches the principle and skips the practice. A lot of training is a video and a quiz. Then the first time someone handles an angry client, a missed deadline or a stakeholder digging in their heels, it's the first time they've ever done it. And it's for real.

Generic training has a second problem. The case studies come from industries you're not in, with stakes your business doesn't carry. People can tell, and they tune out.

## Can AI role-play actually make a manager better?

Yes, if it's built around your company and it tells people exactly what better sounds like. An AI character can play the upset client, the underperforming employee or the tough buyer, and it can adapt to every answer instead of reading from a script. People can practice by voice or by typing, as many times as they need, with no audience in the room.

The scoring matters as much as the conversation. A score alone is just another number. What helps is seeing the stronger answer written out next to your own. Now the coaching starts from what great looks like, not from "you got a 6."

## Why does role-play training feel so awkward?

Because it usually happens in front of coworkers, with a colleague doing a bad impression of a difficult customer. Nobody's at their best with an audience, and the "customer" tends to go easy because they work down the hall from you.

Take the audience away and most of the awkwardness goes with it. Practice at your own pace, against a character that reacts to exactly what you say, at the difficulty you pick.

## What should a new manager practice first?

The conversations that go wrong most often in your business. For most teams that's some version of these:

1. Telling someone their work isn't where it needs to be.
2. Saying no to a client without losing the client.
3. Handling an escalated complaint.
4. Resetting a deadline someone missed.
5. Pushing back on a senior stakeholder.

Start each one easy, then turn up the difficulty. The goal isn't a perfect score. It's making the real conversation feel like the fifth time, not the first.

## What should a manager look for in the results?

Patterns, not single scores. Which skill is the whole team weakest at? Is that getting better over time? Read the actual transcript, because coaching works best when it's about one specific moment, not a number.
